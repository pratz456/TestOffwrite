Tax Court exists in a strange corner of the legal world where everyday people argue with the federal government over specific numbers, often without a lawyer, in proceedings that are surprisingly accessible and occasionally absurd.

These are five real cases involving self-employed workers. The outcomes are instructive, occasionally surprising, and in one case genuinely satisfying.

## Case 1: The Dog Trainer Who Tried to Write Off Her Dogs

**Kathleen Bagley** was a professional dog trainer and show handler. She owned several dogs, competed in shows, and claimed deductions for food, veterinary care, and training costs. The IRS said her dog activities were a hobby, not a business.

The Tax Court sided with Bagley. She had a separate business checking account, kept meticulous records, made consistent efforts to improve her methods, had earned income from training services, and operated in an obviously businesslike manner. The nine factors weighed in her favor.

**The lesson:** Passion-adjacent activities (dogs, horses, art, photography) get extra scrutiny. Businesslike operation and genuine records are what separate a deductible business from a disallowed hobby.

## Case 2: The Travel Blogger Who Deducted Everything

A travel blogger claimed deductions for luxury hotels, business-class flights, and fine dining in over a dozen countries, arguing all of it was content research for her blog. Revenue: approximately $12,000 per year. Total deductions: significantly more.

The Tax Court disallowed most of it. The court found that while some travel may have been business-related, the scale and luxury of the spending bore no rational relationship to the revenue being generated. The blogger had not demonstrated she expected to be profitable or was making real changes to become so.

The 50% rule for meals was applied to the small amount of clearly business-connected dining. The international first-class flights to destinations where only a few posts were published were largely disallowed.

**The lesson:** Deductions need to be proportionate to your actual business. Claiming $80,000 in expenses against $12,000 in revenue from a lifestyle-adjacent activity is a reliable way to end up in Tax Court.

## Case 3: The Musician Who Actually Won

A jazz musician paid dues to his musicians union, bought reeds and strings, paid for rehearsal space, and took masterclasses to maintain his professional level. He also had a day job. The IRS argued his music was a hobby because it generated inconsistent income.

The Tax Court disagreed. The musician had performed professionally for decades. He maintained memberships in professional organizations. He kept music-related records and receipts. He practiced regularly and sought professional instruction to improve. The occasional slow years were attributable to the inherently unpredictable nature of performance work, not lack of profit intent.

All expenses were allowed.

**The lesson:** Long-term professional activity with credible business practices survives hobby classification even through lean years. Context and history matter.

## Case 4: The Consultant Who Forgot That Personal Trips Are Personal

A management consultant deducted airfare to Las Vegas as a business travel expense, citing a conference he attended on the first day of a five-day trip. He also deducted all his meals and hotel for the entire five days.

The Tax Court allowed the airfare and the first-day hotel and meal. The remaining four days, spent at the pool and casino tables rather than at business events, were disallowed as personal. His assertion that he "networked" throughout the week did not satisfy the business purpose requirement.

**The lesson:** When a business trip extends for personal reasons, only the genuinely business portions are deductible. The proportional day method applies to both lodging and meals. No amount of claimed networking saves days that are clearly personal.

## Case 5: The Professional Gambler and the Surprisingly Nuanced Outcome

**Robert Mayo** was a professional poker player who filed Schedule C, reporting gambling winnings as business income and deducting gambling losses as business expenses. The IRS argued gambling could not be a trade or business.

The Tax Court found that professional gambling can indeed constitute a trade or business when conducted with continuity and regularity and with the primary purpose of making a profit. Mayo had played poker full-time for years, maintained records, and had periods of significant profit.

However, the court also found that he could not deduct losses in excess of his winnings. Gambling losses offset gambling winnings but cannot create a net loss on Schedule C. The professional gambler can report the income on Schedule C and deduct ordinary business expenses (entry fees, travel to tournaments) but cannot use excess losses to offset other income.

**The lesson:** Unusual activities can qualify as legitimate businesses. But the rules still apply, and some expense categories have specific limitations that survive the business classification.

## The Common Thread

Reading through Tax Court cases involving self-employed workers reveals a consistent pattern. Taxpayers who kept real records, operated genuinely businesslike, and had credible explanations for their deductions generally prevailed. Taxpayers who claimed large deductions against minimal revenue, mixed personal and business spending liberally, and produced weak documentation when asked to explain themselves did not.

The Tax Court is not hostile to self-employed workers or small business owners. It is hostile to deductions that were invented after the fact to reduce taxes rather than incurred genuinely to run a business.

Good records and honest bookkeeping are not just bureaucratic obligations. They are what stand between your deductions and a judge's pen.
