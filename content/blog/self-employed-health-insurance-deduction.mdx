Health insurance is one of the most significant expenses freelancers face : and one of the most powerful deductions available to them. If you're self-employed and pay for your own health coverage, you may be able to deduct **100% of your premiums** directly from your adjusted gross income, reducing your federal income tax (though not your self-employment tax) dollar for dollar.

Here's exactly how this deduction works, who qualifies, and the important interaction with ACA subsidies that trips up many self-employed workers.

## What the Deduction Covers

The self-employed health insurance deduction (**IRC Section 162(l)**) lets eligible self-employed individuals deduct premiums paid for:

- **Medical insurance** : individual or family coverage
- **Dental insurance** : for yourself, spouse, and dependents
- **Vision insurance**
- **Long-term care insurance** : subject to age-based annual limits:
 - Age 40 or younger: $480
 - Ages 41 to 50: $900
 - Ages 51 to 60: $1,800
 - Ages 61 to 70: $4,810
 - Age 71+: $6,000
 (These limits are indexed for inflation; verify current figures each year)

This is an **above-the-line deduction** : it reduces your adjusted gross income (AGI) on **Schedule 1, Line 17**, not on Schedule C. That matters because your AGI affects many other calculations, including eligibility for other credits and deductions.

## Who Qualifies

To claim the self-employed health insurance deduction, you must meet all of these conditions:

1. **You must be self-employed** : a sole proprietor, LLC owner, partner, or more-than-2% S-corporation shareholder with net profit from the business

2. **You must not be eligible for subsidized coverage through an employer** : specifically, you cannot claim this deduction for any month in which you or your spouse were **eligible** (not just enrolled) for coverage through an employer's group plan

3. **Your deduction cannot exceed your net profit** from the business : the deduction is limited to the net profit from the business under which the insurance was established

The employer eligibility rule is the most commonly misunderstood. If your spouse is offered family coverage through their employer, even if you declined it because your freelance plan is better or cheaper , you are generally ineligible for the self-employed health insurance deduction for those months. The IRS considers you "eligible for coverage" the moment an employer's plan is available to you, whether you take it or not.

**Month-by-month**: The eligibility test is applied each month. If you start a W-2 job in September and become eligible for employer coverage, you can still claim the self-employed health insurance deduction for January through August.

## Calculating the Deduction

The calculation is straightforward if you qualify:

**Step 1**: Total all premiums you paid during the year for qualifying coverage.

**Step 2**: Subtract any **Premium Tax Credit (PTC)** you received (this is the ACA marketplace subsidy : more on this below). You can only deduct the portion you actually paid out of pocket.

**Step 3**: Compare to your net profit from the business. The deduction cannot exceed your net self-employment profit.

**Example**: You paid $12,000 in health insurance premiums, your Schedule C shows $80,000 in net profit, and you received no PTC.
- Deductible amount: $12,000 (limited to net profit of $80,000, so full $12,000 applies)

**Example 2**: Same facts, but you received a $4,000 Premium Tax Credit.
- Deductible amount: $8,000 ($12,000 premiums − $4,000 PTC)

## The ACA Marketplace and Premium Tax Credit Interaction

This is where many freelancers get tripped up, because the self-employed health insurance deduction and the ACA **Premium Tax Credit (PTC)** interact in a circular way.

Here's the issue:
- Your PTC is based on your household income (AGI)
- Your self-employed health insurance deduction reduces your AGI
- A lower AGI increases your PTC
- A higher PTC reduces your deduction
- Which lowers your AGI further, which increases your PTC…

The IRS resolves this circularity with an iterative calculation. In practice, most people going through tax software or working with a CPA resolve this automatically : but it means the deduction and credit amounts are **interdependent** and must be calculated together, not separately.

**The practical upshot**: If you buy coverage on the ACA marketplace and receive an advance PTC, you must reconcile it on **Form 8962** when you file. The amount you actually deduct on Schedule 1 will be the premiums you paid minus the final PTC amount.

**Planning consideration**: Your income estimate when applying for marketplace coverage affects your advance PTC. Self-employed income is volatile and often hard to predict. If you underestimate your income and receive too large an advance PTC, you'll repay the excess when you file. If you overestimate, you'll get the difference back as a credit. Updating your income estimate with the marketplace during the year if your income changes significantly can minimize surprises.

## S-Corporation Owners (More Than 2% Shareholders)

If you operate as an S-corp, the health insurance deduction works slightly differently. A more-than-2% S-corp shareholder who wants to claim the deduction must:

1. Have the S-corp **pay the premiums or reimburse you** for them
2. Have the premium amount **included in your W-2 wages** as compensation (but not subject to FICA taxes)
3. Then deduct it on your personal return on Schedule 1

If the S-corp doesn't include the premiums in your W-2, you lose the deduction entirely. This is a compliance requirement that catches many S-corp owners off guard : work with your payroll provider to ensure premiums are correctly reported on your annual W-2.

## HSA Contributions: The Second Health-Related Deduction

If your health plan is a **High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP)**, you can also contribute to a **Health Savings Account (HSA)** : and those contributions are deductible on **Schedule 1, Line 13**, completely separate from the self-employed health insurance deduction.

**2026 HSA limits**:
- Individual coverage: **$4,300**
- Family coverage: **$8,550**
- Catch-up contribution (age 55+): Additional **$1,000**

HSA contributions reduce your AGI, grow tax-free, and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified medical expenses : including the premiums themselves in some circumstances. For self-employed workers with high healthcare expenses, maxing out an HSA is one of the most powerful tax strategies available.

## Marketplace vs. Private Insurance: Does It Matter?

The self-employed health insurance deduction applies to coverage purchased through any qualified source:
- ACA marketplace (Healthcare.gov or state exchanges)
- Directly from an insurance company
- Through a professional association or group plan
- COBRA continuation coverage (if you're self-employed now but the coverage originated from a prior employer)

The source doesn't affect deductibility. What matters is that you paid the premiums and you qualified for the deduction in those months.

## What You Can't Deduct Here (But May Still Be Able to Deduct)

The self-employed health insurance deduction doesn't cover all medical expenses. Out-of-pocket costs : copays, deductibles, prescriptions, dental work : are not included here. However, they may still be deductible as **itemized medical expenses on Schedule A** to the extent they exceed **7.5% of your AGI**.

If your medical costs are substantial and you itemize, the combination of the health insurance deduction (above-the-line) and excess medical expenses (Schedule A) can provide meaningful tax relief.
