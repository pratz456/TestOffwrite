Every tax season, the same pattern emerges: the same mistakes, the same missed deductions, the same avoidable penalties. Not from carelessness, but from not knowing the rules. Here are the seven that show up most often and what each one actually costs.

## Mistake 1: Letting Platform Fees Disappear

Your Stripe dashboard shows $95,000 processed last year. Stripe's 1099-K shows $95,000. Your Schedule C shows $95,000 in gross receipts and nothing on the fees line. Looks right.

It is incomplete.

Stripe charged processing fees on every transaction (its published US card rate is 2.9% plus 30 cents per charge). On $95,000 in volume, that is roughly $2,855 you never received: $2,755 from the 2.9%, plus 30 cents on each of about 330 charges. Gross receipts on Schedule C Line 1 should still reconcile to the 1099-K, because the IRS matches those forms against your return - but the fees belong on Line 10 (Commissions and Fees) as a deduction. Skip that line and you pay tax on money you never had; report only the net and you invite a matching notice for the difference.

The same applies to Etsy transaction fees, DoorDash commissions, Upwork service fees, and every other platform that reports gross volume on 1099-Ks.

**What it costs:** Forgetting $2,855 of fees costs roughly $870 in unnecessary tax for someone in the 22% bracket (about 30.5% once SE tax and the QBI deduction are counted).

## Mistake 2: Not Deducting the Home Office Because "It Might Trigger an Audit"

The fear is real. The logic is backward.

If part of your home is used regularly and exclusively for your business, and it is your principal place of business (or where you regularly meet clients, or a separate structure), you can generally claim the home office deduction. The simplified method gives you $5 per square foot for up to 300 square feet ($1,500), and the deduction can't exceed the income from the business. A 200-square-foot office yields $1,000 in deductions, roughly $300 in tax savings in the 22% bracket.

The IRS does not publish audit rates for home office claims, but a legitimate, well-documented claim is nothing to fear. The cost of not claiming it is certain.

**What it costs:** Under the simplified method (at most $1,500), a home office saves up to about $460 a year in the 22% bracket. The regular method, using actual expenses on Form 8829, can be worth more. Over five years of skipping the simplified deduction: up to about $2,300.

## Mistake 3: Missing the Self-Employed Health Insurance Deduction

You pay $650/month for health coverage. That is $7,800 per year. For each month you were not eligible for a subsidized health plan through your employer, your spouse's employer, or the employer of your dependent or child under 27, the premiums for that month are generally deductible above the line, up to your net profit from the business. If you bought Marketplace coverage with a premium tax credit, the calculation changes (see IRS Pub. 974).

This deduction does not go on Schedule C. It goes on Schedule 1, Line 17. It is easy to miss in software if you rush through the interview or do not recognize the question. The result: $7,800 in deductions left unclaimed.

**What it costs:** In the 22% bracket, a missed $7,800 health insurance deduction costs about $1,370 in unnecessary tax (22% of $7,800, less the 20% QBI deduction the health insurance deduction reduces). In the 12% bracket it is about $750.

## Mistake 4: Skipping Quarterly Payments Because "I'll Pay It All in April"

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal tax after withholding and refundable credits, you generally need to make quarterly estimated payments. You avoid the penalty if your withholding and timely estimates cover at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's tax (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000, or $75,000 if married filing separately). Skipping payments does not reduce the tax. It just adds an underpayment penalty when you file.

The penalty is interest at the federal underpayment rate on each installment from its due date until you pay - 7% for all of 2025 and for most 2026 quarters (6% for April–June 2026). On $15,750 in total tax liability with no quarterly payments, the penalty works out to roughly $700. Not devastating, but entirely avoidable.

**What it costs:** Several hundred dollars in penalties annually, plus the stress of a large April payment on income you already spent.

## Mistake 5: Not Tracking Mileage at All

The IRS standard mileage rate for 2026 is 72.5 cents per mile for January through June and 76 cents per mile for July through December (the 2025 rate was 70 cents). A freelancer who drives 8,000 business miles spread across 2026 (client meetings, post office runs, supply pickups, work-related travel) gets about $5,940 in deductions, worth roughly $1,800 in tax savings in the 22% bracket.

Track mileage as you drive. A log rebuilt from memory in March for the previous January carries little weight unless other records back it up. The log takes about 10 seconds per trip.

**What it costs:** A freelancer in the 22% bracket driving 8,000 business miles per year who has no usable log risks losing roughly $1,800 in tax savings annually.

## Mistake 6: Not Contributing to a Retirement Account

A SEP-IRA lets you contribute up to 20% of your net profit after subtracting half of your SE tax (the plan's 25% rate, adjusted for self-employed people), which works out to about 18.6% of Schedule C net profit, capped at $70,000 for 2025 and $72,000 for 2026. Contributions are tax-deductible. Money grows tax-deferred. You can set up and fund a SEP for 2026 as late as your 2026 return due date, including extensions (April 15, 2027, or October 15, 2027 if you file an extension). The deadline for 2025 contributions has passed.

On $80,000 in net SE income, the maximum SEP contribution is about $14,870. A $7,500 contribution saves about $1,320 in federal income tax if you are in the 22% bracket (22% of $7,500, less the 20% QBI deduction it reduces). A single filer with $80,000 of net profit and no other income is in the 12% bracket for 2026, so the saving there is about $720. Retirement contributions reduce income tax but not SE tax. The money is not gone - it is invested for your future, and taxed when you withdraw it in retirement.

**What it costs:** A freelancer in the 22% bracket who skips a $7,500 SEP-IRA contribution for 10 years pays roughly $13,200 in income tax that could have been deferred, and misses a decade of tax-deferred growth.

## Mistake 7: Using One Bank Account for Everything

This is not a tax rule violation. It is a setup that makes it much easier to miss deductions, misclassify personal expenses as business, and spend hours reconstructing records at tax time.

With one mixed account, every transaction requires a judgment call. Three hundred transactions means three hundred judgment calls, under time pressure, at the worst time of year. The error rate is significant and usually runs in one direction: missed deductions.

**What it costs:** There is no official statistic, but the arithmetic is simple. If a mixed-account freelancer overlooks even 15% of $31,500 in legitimate business expenses, that is about $4,700 in missed deductions, worth roughly $1,400 in tax annually in the 22% bracket.

## The Cumulative Math

These seven mistakes together, using the examples above, for a single freelancer whose taxable income is in the 22% bracket (in 2026, roughly $85,000 to $160,000 of net profit with no other income and the standard deduction):
- Forgotten platform fees: $870
- Missing home office: $305
- Missing health insurance deduction: $1,370
- Underpayment penalties: $700
- No mileage tracking: $1,810
- No SEP-IRA (tax deferred, not eliminated): $1,320
- Mixed accounts / missed deductions: $1,440

**Total: roughly $7,800 per year** in this illustration. None of this requires tax sophistication to fix. It requires a separate bank account, a mileage log you actually keep, and 30 minutes to set up a SEP-IRA.
