The word "audit" triggers disproportionate fear relative to actual risk. The IRS audited about 0.38% of individual returns in recent years : less than 1 in 250. But self-employed filers, particularly those with higher Schedule C income, see elevated rates. And certain patterns are reliably more likely to generate attention.

Understanding what actually triggers audits, and what is just urban myth , lets you file accurately and confidently instead of either underreporting out of fear or leaving legitimate deductions on the table.

## What Actually Drives Audit Selection

The IRS uses a formula called the Discriminant Inventory Function (DIF) score to rank returns by audit potential. The inputs are not fully public, but decades of Tax Court cases and IRS guidance reveal the patterns.

**Income that does not match third-party reports:** The single most reliable audit trigger. If your Schedule C shows $45,000 in gross receipts but the IRS received 1099s totaling $52,000, the mismatch generates an automated flag. This is a CP2000, not technically an audit, but it functions similarly and requires the same level of response.

**Cash-intensive industries:** The IRS specifically targets industries where cash transactions are common and underreporting is structurally easy: restaurants, hair salons, car washes, construction subcontractors, and similar businesses. If your industry has a known cash component, expect heightened scrutiny.

**Large losses relative to income:** Reporting $120,000 in business expenses against $60,000 in revenue is a mathematical flag. It does not mean the expenses are wrong : startup years, equipment purchases, and genuinely bad business years produce real losses. But the ratio triggers review.

**Home office deduction:** The home office deduction was historically a major audit trigger when many people claimed it incorrectly. The IRS is more comfortable with it now, particularly as remote work has become normal. Claiming it correctly with real documentation is completely defensible. Claiming a room where you also watch television is not.

**Meals deductions significantly above industry norms:** The DIF score compares your deductions to other filers with similar income and business type. Meals at 30% of revenue when the industry norm is 5% is a flag. Document every meal regardless.

**Round numbers throughout:** Reporting exactly $5,000 in supplies, $10,000 in travel, $3,000 in advertising suggests estimation rather than actual tracking. Real expenses are irregular. $4,837 and $9,122 and $2,645 are more credible than even thousands.

## What Does Not Actually Trigger Audits

**Home office alone:** Correctly documented, exclusively used, regularly maintained : the home office deduction is legitimate and the IRS knows it. The risk is claiming it without meeting the requirements, not claiming it when you do.

**Mileage deduction:** High mileage is expected for drivers, delivery workers, and sales-intensive businesses. The issue is documentation. A log with dates and business purposes is your protection, not avoiding the deduction.

**The QBI deduction:** Some people skip the 20% QBI deduction because they are worried it looks suspicious. This deduction exists by design for exactly this population. File Form 8995 and claim it.

**Self-employed health insurance deduction:** A straightforward deduction that is clearly contemplated by the tax code. Claim it.

**Hiring family members:** Legitimate when compensation is reasonable for actual services performed. The deduction is real; the requirement is that family members actually work and are paid market rates.

## The Three Categories of Audits

**Correspondence audit:** The most common. A letter arrives asking you to explain or document something specific : a deduction, an income discrepancy, a credit. Respond in writing with documentation. Many resolve without any additional tax owed.

**Office audit:** You meet with an IRS agent at an IRS office. Specific items on your return are examined. Bring documentation for everything related to the items in question, not everything on your return.

**Field audit:** An agent comes to your place of business. Rare, reserved for complex or high-dollar situations. If this happens, get professional representation immediately.

## How to Make Your Return Audit-Resistant

**Match income to third-party reporting.** If 1099s were issued totaling $80,000, report at least $80,000 in Schedule C gross receipts. Explain legitimately lower amounts (fees, returns, prior-year income) with clear documentation.

**Document all deductions at the time they occur.** The contemporaneous record standard means written at the time, not reconstructed later. A receipt plus a note made the same day is significantly stronger than a receipt plus memory six months later.

**Keep business and personal expenses completely separate.** A dedicated business account and business credit card create a clean record by default. Mixed accounts require transaction-by-transaction analysis that creates both work and risk.

**Be consistent year to year.** Dramatic changes in deduction patterns : home office claimed some years but not others, mileage that doubles without explanation : create questions. Consistency reads as routine.

**Claim what you actually incurred.** The goal is not to minimize your deductions because you are afraid of attention. The goal is accurate reporting with documentation. Legitimate deductions with real records are exactly what the tax code contemplates.
