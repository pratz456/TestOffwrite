The envelope is from the IRS and it does not look like the usual junk mail. Inside is a CP2000 notice with a proposed tax bill that may be anywhere from a few hundred dollars to several thousand. Your stomach drops.

Here is the first thing to understand: **a CP2000 is not an audit.** It is an automated letter from the IRS's computer matching program. The IRS received information from third parties (employers, banks, clients, payment processors) and that information does not match your return. They are asking you to explain the discrepancy or pay the difference.

It is a problem. It is a solvable problem.

## What Triggers a CP2000

The IRS receives copies of 1099-NEC forms, 1099-K forms, W-2s, 1099-INT, 1099-DIV, and other information returns from every payer in the country. Their computers cross-reference all of it against what you reported.

Common triggers for freelancers:

**Unreported 1099 income.** A client filed a 1099-NEC showing they paid you $5,000. You forgot to include that client on your Schedule C, or you reported the income but under a different line. The computer flags the discrepancy.

**1099-K income not reconciled.** Stripe, PayPal, or another processor reported gross payment volume to you. If you reported net income after fees rather than gross receipts, the numbers do not match. You may owe nothing extra, but you have to show the math.

**Double-counted income.** A client paid via PayPal and issued a 1099-NEC for the same payments. You reported it once (correctly) but the IRS computer sees it twice and proposes tax on both.

**Unreported interest, dividends, or investment income.** A 1099-INT from your bank that was easy to miss.

## The Proposed Bill Is Not Final

This is the critical thing most people do not understand about CP2000 notices. The proposed amount is not a bill. It is a preliminary assessment the IRS is asking you to either confirm, dispute, or explain.

You have **60 days** from the date on the notice to respond. That date is on the notice, not the date you received it. Open these letters immediately.

## How to Respond

**Step 1: Figure out if they are right.**

Pull your original return, the 1099s or other documents the IRS mentions, and your own records. Three possible outcomes:

- You missed income that was actually taxable. You owe some or all of what they proposed.
- You reported the income correctly but in a way their computers did not recognize. You owe nothing but need to explain.
- The 1099 the IRS received is wrong, or it was already accounted for somewhere else on your return.

**Step 2: Respond in writing before the deadline.**

Never ignore a CP2000. Ignoring it means the IRS will assess the proposed amount, add penalties and interest, and start collection. The notice becomes a much larger and harder problem.

**If you agree with the IRS:** Sign and return the response form with payment or payment arrangements.

**If you partially agree:** Send a signed agreement for the correct amount with a written explanation of why the rest of the proposed amount is wrong.

**If you disagree entirely:** Send a written response explaining why, with supporting documentation. This is where having your records organized pays dividends.

## The Documentation That Wins

For freelancers, the most useful documents are your complete Schedule C as filed, all 1099s you received, bank or payment processor records showing what you actually received, and records of any fees, refunds, or offsets that explain discrepancies between 1099 amounts and reported income.

If a payment processor reported $40,000 in gross volume but you only reported $36,000 as income, a statement showing $4,000 in fees and chargebacks that reduced your net receipts is your entire response.

## The Costly Mistake

People receive a CP2000, see a number that feels wrong, get frustrated, and put the letter aside intending to deal with it later. Then they miss the 60-day window.

Once the deadline passes without a response, the IRS sends a Notice of Deficiency (CP3219). At that point, options narrow significantly. You can petition the Tax Court within 90 days of the Notice of Deficiency, but now you are in court proceedings rather than a simple letter exchange.

The CP2000 process is designed to be resolved through correspondence. Keep it there.

## Getting Help

CP2000 notices with small proposed amounts and clear factual explanations are routinely handled without professional help. Write a clear response, include your documentation, and send it certified mail with a return receipt.

Notices with large proposed amounts, complex situations, or years of unreported income are worth at least a consultation with a CPA or tax attorney. The cost of professional help on a well-defined problem is predictable. The cost of an unresolved IRS matter is not.
