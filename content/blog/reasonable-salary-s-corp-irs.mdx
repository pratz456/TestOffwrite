The S-corporation strategy is genuinely attractive. Pay yourself a salary, pay FICA taxes on that salary, take the rest as a distribution that's not subject to self-employment tax. At $150,000 in net income with a $70,000 salary, you save around $12,000 in self-employment taxes.

There is, however, a trap built directly into this strategy. The IRS has been aware of it for decades and has an entire audit program dedicated to it. The trap is called the **reasonable salary requirement**, and failing to navigate it turns a clever tax strategy into an expensive problem.

## Why S-Corps Have a Salary Requirement at All

The whole point of the S-corp tax strategy is that distributions aren't subject to self-employment tax, but wages are. The obvious temptation: pay yourself the minimum possible salary, take the rest as distributions, and keep most of your income free from the 15.3% SE tax bite.

The IRS saw this coming. The rule: **S-corp owner-employees must pay themselves a reasonable salary** for services they perform for the corporation. Not a token salary. Not whatever number minimizes their taxes. A salary comparable to what they'd pay someone else to do the same work.

Ignore this rule and the IRS can reclassify your distributions as wages, hit you with back payroll taxes plus penalties plus interest, and potentially assess accuracy-related penalties on top of all of it.

## Real Audits, Real Numbers

This isn't theoretical risk. The IRS has an active audit program targeting S-corps with suspiciously low officer compensation.

One famous case: a tax attorney running an S-corp paid himself a $24,000 salary while taking over $200,000 in distributions. The IRS reclassified most of the distributions as wages. The back taxes, penalties, and interest were substantial, and the case became a textbook example used in IRS training materials.

The IRS has also gone after professionals in a wide range of fields: financial advisors, consultants, physicians, accountants, engineers. Anyone generating meaningful income through personal services in an S-corp structure is on their radar.

## What "Reasonable" Actually Means

The IRS doesn't define a specific number. They look at what someone in your role, with your experience, would earn as an employee doing the same work in the same market.

Relevant factors:

- **What similar professionals earn.** A freelance software developer billing $150/hour should be paying themselves a salary consistent with what software developers earn in their market.
- **What the company can afford.** If the business had a bad year, a reduced salary can be justified.
- **The amount of time and effort you contribute.** Someone working part-time in the business can justify a lower salary than someone working full-time.
- **Your training and experience.** Senior-level professionals command higher salaries.
- **Geographic market rates.** Salaries vary by location.

The IRS uses BLS (Bureau of Labor Statistics) wage data, comparable company salary surveys, and expert testimony from compensation specialists in court cases.

## The Most Common Mistake

Paying yourself a round-number salary that's clearly calculated to minimize taxes rather than to reflect fair compensation.

A software consultant billing $300,000 per year to clients, paying themselves a $30,000 salary and taking $270,000 in distributions: the IRS finds this implausible. Software consultants don't earn $30,000. The salary exists to minimize taxes, not to reflect reality.

A freelance marketing consultant earning $90,000 and paying themselves a $60,000 salary, taking $30,000 as a distribution: this is more defensible. The salary is meaningful, it's roughly consistent with marketing manager compensation in many markets, and the tax savings are modest.

## How to Set a Defensible Salary

**Do the research.** Look up Bureau of Labor Statistics data for your occupation. Check industry salary surveys, LinkedIn salary data, job postings in your area. Document what you found and why you chose your salary.

**Pay yourself at least 40-50% of S-corp net income** as salary if you're the primary service provider. Some tax advisors suggest higher percentages. The exact number is less important than having a documented, reasoned basis.

**Update your salary as income changes.** If your S-corp nets $200,000 one year and $80,000 the next, your salary should reflect that reality. A fixed low salary regardless of business income looks like tax avoidance rather than compensation.

**Keep contemporaneous records.** Document your job duties, hours worked, and the research you did to set your salary. If the IRS asks, you want paper showing you thought about this seriously, not just grabbed a number.

## The Cost of Getting It Wrong

If the IRS reclassifies your distributions as wages:

- You owe the employee and employer shares of FICA taxes on the reclassified amount
- Late deposit penalties for payroll taxes not withheld
- Failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties for late payroll tax returns
- Interest on all of the above from the date the taxes were originally due

In a bad scenario, a few years of undercompensation can generate a bill that significantly exceeds the tax savings you achieved during that time.

The S-corp strategy works. It just requires treating yourself like an actual employee, which means paying yourself what you'd pay a qualified stranger to do your job. That's not a loophole. It's the whole deal.
