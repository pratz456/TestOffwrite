Every April, homeowners near Augusta National Golf Club rent out their homes to Masters Tournament attendees for enormous sums. Congress, being Congress, decided this income should not be taxable. The result was a law that applies to every homeowner in America, not just people lucky enough to live on the 18th fairway.

Section 280A(g) of the tax code is now known as the Augusta Rule, and it creates a genuinely useful strategy for self-employed workers who own a home.

## The Basic Idea

If you rent your home for **14 days or fewer per year**, the rental income is completely excluded from your gross income. You do not report it. You do not pay tax on it. It simply does not exist for federal tax purposes.

Now here is where it gets interesting for freelancers and small business owners.

## Renting to Your Own Business

If you operate as an S-corporation, C-corporation, or partnership, your business entity is legally separate from you as an individual. That means your business can rent your home as a meeting venue, and you can collect tax-free rental income from that transaction.

The setup looks like this: your S-corp pays you, the homeowner, a fair market rental rate to hold a business meeting at your home. The business deducts the expense (it is a legitimate business cost). You receive the rental income completely tax-free under the 14-day exclusion.

Used correctly, this converts money from your S-corp into your personal pocket without payroll taxes or income taxes on that portion.

## The Real Numbers

Suppose the fair market rental rate for your home is $1,500 per day for a business event. Your S-corp holds 10 management meetings there during the year. The business pays you $15,000 in rental fees.

Your S-corp: deducts $15,000 as a business meeting expense.
You personally: receive $15,000 in rental income that is fully excluded from your gross income.

At a 30% combined tax rate, that is $4,500 in tax savings versus pulling the same money as salary or distribution.

## What Makes It Legitimate

The IRS expects this to be a real transaction, not a paperwork fiction. That means several things need to be true.

**Fair market rate.** You must charge what an arm's-length third party would actually pay to rent your home for business purposes. Research comparable venue rentals in your area. Conference rooms, event spaces, and meeting venues all provide comparables. Do not make up a number.

**Real business purpose.** The meetings held at your home must be genuine business activities: strategy sessions, board meetings, planning retreats, client presentations. These need to actually occur and have business substance.

**Written rental agreement.** Document the arrangement in writing, including the dates, purpose, rate, and parties involved. Treat it like any other business contract.

**Business actually pays.** The money must actually transfer from the business account to your personal account. No side deals, no netting, no informal arrangements.

**14 days maximum.** Exceed 14 days and the income becomes taxable. Track the count carefully.

## Who Can Use This

This strategy works when you have a separate legal entity (S-corp, C-corp, or partnership) that can serve as the tenant. Sole proprietors cannot use it because there is no separate entity to pay rent to yourself. You and your business are legally the same person.

If you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity, the Augusta Rule does not apply to a self-rental arrangement.

## What the IRS Pays Attention To

Auditors look for three things when this comes up: whether the rate was genuinely at fair market value, whether the meetings actually happened and had real business content, and whether the 14-day limit was respected.

The most common mistake is using inflated rental rates. If your home would rent for $300 per day for a gathering, billing $3,000 per day because you want more tax-free income is asking for trouble. The rate needs to be defensible against actual comparables.

## The Documentation You Need

Keep a rental agreement signed by both the business and you as homeowner. Document each meeting with an agenda, attendee list, and date. Keep records of payment. Maintain the comparables you used to determine the rental rate.

This is not a complicated paper trail. A few documents per meeting and a business checking account record is the whole thing. The effort is small relative to the tax benefit.

The Augusta Rule is one of the clearest examples in the tax code where a provision intended for one situation (rich people near a golf tournament) turned out to apply universally and create legitimate planning opportunities for everyone. Not many tax strategies are this clean.
