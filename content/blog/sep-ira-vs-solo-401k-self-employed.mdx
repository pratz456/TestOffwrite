Most freelancers think about retirement accounts the way they think about dental appointments : something they should probably do but keep putting off. This is an expensive habit.

Self-employed retirement contributions directly reduce your adjusted gross income. Every dollar you put in is a dollar the IRS cannot tax this year. At a 22% income tax rate plus 14% SE tax, a $10,000 SEP-IRA contribution saves you $3,600 in taxes. That is not a refund. That is money that never gets taken.

Here is how the two main options compare.

## SEP-IRA: Simple, Flexible, High Limits

A SEP-IRA (Simplified Employee Pension) is the easiest self-employed retirement account to set up and maintain. Open one at any brokerage in about 15 minutes. No annual filing requirements beyond your tax return.

**Contribution limit:** 25% of your net self-employment income, up to $70,000 for 2025. The IRS defines this precisely as 20% of your Schedule C net profit after the SE tax deduction.

**The actual math:**
Net SE income: $80,000
SE tax: $80,000 × 0.9235 × 0.153 = $11,305
Half SE deduction: $5,652
Net profit for SEP calculation: $80,000 − $5,652 = $74,348
Maximum SEP contribution: $74,348 × 0.20 = $14,870

**Deadline:** You can fund a SEP-IRA up until your tax return due date, including extensions. File for an October extension and you have until October 15 to contribute for the prior year. This flexibility is the SEP's biggest advantage over the Solo 401(k).

**Best for:** Sole proprietors who want simplicity, variable income (fund more in good years), or who need to make contributions after year-end.

## Solo 401(k): Higher Contributions for the Right Situation

A Solo 401(k), also called an Individual 401(k) or Self-Employed 401(k) , has a more complex contribution structure but can allow significantly higher contributions at lower income levels.

**Two contribution buckets:**

**Employee contribution (elective deferral):** Up to $23,500 for 2025 ($31,000 if you are 50 or older). You can contribute this full amount even with relatively low net income, as long as you earned at least that much.

**Employer contribution:** Same 20% of net SE income formula as the SEP-IRA.

Combined limit: $70,000 for 2025 (same overall cap as SEP-IRA at high income levels).

**Where the Solo 401(k) wins:** At lower income levels. If your net SE income is $50,000, a SEP-IRA lets you contribute $9,394. A Solo 401(k) lets you contribute $23,500 as an employee deferral plus $9,394 as the employer contribution : $32,894 total. More than three times as much.

At income above roughly $230,000, the SEP-IRA and Solo 401(k) reach the same $70,000 cap.

**The catch:** Solo 401(k) plans must be **established by December 31** of the tax year, even if you fund them later. Miss that deadline and you cannot create one retroactively for that year. Once assets exceed $250,000, you must file Form 5500-EZ annually.

## Roth Option

Solo 401(k)s can offer a Roth component where you contribute after-tax dollars that grow and withdraw tax-free. SEP-IRAs are traditional-only (pre-tax contributions, taxable withdrawals). If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement than you are now, the Roth Solo 401(k) option has value.

## What If You Also Have a W-2 Job?

If you have a day job with a 401(k) and a self-employed side income:

The $23,500 employee contribution limit applies across all 401(k)s combined. If you maxed your employer's 401(k), you cannot make additional employee deferrals to a Solo 401(k). But you can still make the employer contribution portion, the 20% of net SE income , to the Solo 401(k). This is often still worth opening.

A SEP-IRA has no interaction with your W-2 401(k) at all. You can max both independently.

## The Tax Math on a Real Example

Freelance designer. Net SE income: $90,000. Contributes $18,000 to SEP-IRA.

Without the contribution:
- SE tax: ~$12,730
- Taxable income: ~$77,270 (after SE deduction and standard deduction)
- Income tax: ~$13,000
- Total: ~$25,730

With the $18,000 SEP-IRA contribution:
- SE tax: same $12,730 (SE tax is not reduced by retirement contributions)
- Taxable income: ~$59,270
- Income tax: ~$9,041
- Total: ~$21,771

The $18,000 contribution saved $3,959 in taxes. The money is not spent : it is invested for retirement. Effective cost of putting $18,000 into retirement: $14,041.

Open one. Fund it. Do it before December 31 if you want a Solo 401(k), or before your return due date if a SEP-IRA. The tax math on self-employed retirement accounts is one of the best deals in the entire tax code.
