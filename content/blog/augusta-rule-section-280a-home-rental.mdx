Augusta, Georgia has a famous golf tournament. It also has an unexpectedly interesting tax history. For years, homeowners near Augusta National rented their houses to wealthy spectators during Masters week for enormous sums, then collected the rent completely tax-free. Congress, apparently deciding that wealthy golf fans deserved a break, codified the rule. The result is **Section 280A(g)** of the tax code, now nicknamed the Augusta Rule.

What it says: if you rent your home for **14 days or fewer per year**, you don't have to report that rental income at all. The money is completely excluded from your gross income.

Most people who know this rule use it to rent their house during a local sporting event or concert. But self-employed business owners found a more interesting application.

## The Strategy

Here's how it works for a freelancer or small business owner:

1. Your business (LLC, S-corp, or even sole prop) needs space for meetings, retreats, planning sessions, or other legitimate business activities.
2. Your home has suitable space for those activities.
3. Your business **pays you rent** for using your home on those occasions.
4. You collect up to 14 days of rent as completely tax-free income.
5. Your business deducts the rent as an ordinary business expense.

The result: money moves from your business (deductible) to you personally (tax-free). Done correctly with the right documentation, this is entirely legal.

## What "Correct Documentation" Actually Means

The IRS takes a hard look at transactions between a business and its owner. The Augusta Rule works, but only when you treat it like a real arms-length rental.

**Determine a legitimate market rate.** Look up what similar spaces rent for in your area for similar purposes. Conference rooms in your city, hotel meeting spaces, or Peerspace hourly rentals set the benchmark. You need to pay market rates, not whatever number is most convenient for your taxes.

**Formal rental agreement.** Create a written lease or rental agreement between your business and you as homeowner. It needs to specify the dates, the space being rented, the rate, and the purpose.

**Business purpose documentation.** Keep records showing the business actually used your home on those dates: meeting notes, attendee lists, agendas, calendar records.

**Pay via business check or bank transfer.** The money needs to actually move from the business account to your personal account.

**Two separate entities help.** This strategy is most defensible when your business is structured as an S-corp or LLC rather than a sole proprietorship. When the business and the owner are legally the same person (sole prop), the IRS can argue it's just moving money between pockets. A separate legal entity makes the transaction more legitimate.

## The Numbers

Suppose you live in a mid-size city where renting a comparable space for a business meeting runs $500 per day. Your S-corp rents your home for 14 days of strategy sessions, retreats, and team meetings at $500/day.

- Business deducts: $7,000
- You receive: $7,000
- Tax on that $7,000 income: $0

At a combined federal and state rate of 35%, your business just saved $2,450 in taxes, and you pocketed $7,000 tax-free.

The higher the market rate for space in your area, the more powerful this strategy becomes. In major cities where conference space runs $1,000-2,000 per day, 14 days generates a tax-free windfall of meaningful size.

## The 14-Day Hard Limit

The moment you hit day 15, the entire exemption disappears. All rental income becomes taxable. This isn't a sliding scale. It's a cliff.

If you rent your home for 15 days, you pay tax on all 15 days' worth of rent, not just the income from the 15th day. Track your days carefully.

## What This Strategy Is Not

This is not a license to call every hour you work from home a "business rental day." The IRS requires actual business use of the space, actual meetings or activities, actual documentation. Reconstructed records created months later when someone told you about this strategy are a different and much more uncomfortable situation.

The Augusta Rule is a legitimate tool that Congress intentionally built into the tax code. Use it correctly and it's a genuine annual benefit. Use it sloppily and it becomes an audit adventure you didn't sign up for.

The lesson of Augusta: sometimes the most interesting tax planning comes from Congress trying to help wealthy people rent their homes during golf tournaments. The rest of us just happened to notice.
