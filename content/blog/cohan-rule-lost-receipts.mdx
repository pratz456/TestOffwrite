George M. Cohan was the legendary Broadway composer behind "Give My Regards to Broadway" and "You're a Grand Old Flag." He was also a man who spent money like it was going out of style and kept receipts like they were radioactive.

When the IRS audited him in the 1920s, Cohan could not document thousands of dollars in claimed business expenses. The IRS disallowed everything. Cohan appealed, and in 1930, the Second Circuit Court of Appeals ruled in his favor with a principle that still applies today.

The court reasoned that it was obvious Cohan had incurred real business expenses. Disallowing them entirely because of missing paperwork would be allowing a form to triumph over substance. The court permitted him to estimate his deductions based on available evidence, even without receipts.

That decision became the **Cohan Rule**, and it has been saving imperfect recordkeepers ever since.

## What the Cohan Rule Actually Says

When a taxpayer cannot substantiate an expense with documentation but there is credible evidence the expense occurred, the Tax Court can allow a reasonable estimate of the deductible amount.

This is not a free pass. The rule has real limits. But it does mean that losing your records is not automatically a death sentence for your deductions.

## When It Works

The Cohan Rule tends to work when the nature of the expense is clear and plausible given the taxpayer's business, there is some corroborating evidence that the expense occurred even without a receipt, and the estimated amount is reasonable and conservative.

A freelance photographer who can show client invoices, a portfolio of work, and records of equipment purchases over the years can make a credible case for estimated film, supply, or maintenance expenses from a year where detailed records were lost.

A consultant who can demonstrate travel for specific client engagements through client invoices, calendar records, and credit card statements showing charges in the right cities can support estimated meal and lodging costs even without every individual receipt.

The more objective evidence you have that the expenses were real, the better your position.

## The Four Categories Where It Flat Out Does Not Work

Congress explicitly carved out four expense types from the Cohan Rule in Section 274(d). For these, you either have the documentation or you get nothing. No estimates, no reasonable approximations.

**1. Travel expenses.** Airfare, hotels, and related costs require receipts and records of business purpose.

**2. Business meals.** Must document amount, date, place, business purpose, and attendees.

**3. Entertainment expenses.** (Now largely irrelevant since entertainment deductions were eliminated post-2018, but technically still on the list.)

**4. Listed property.** This category includes passenger automobiles, computers, cell phones, and other items with high personal use potential. Vehicle mileage logs, business use percentages, and acquisition records are required.

The IRS put these categories in the no-estimation zone specifically because they are the most frequently abused. They are also the categories most freelancers care about most.

## What You Can Do When Records Are Gone

**Reconstruct what you can.** Bank statements, credit card statements, and PayPal records are often retrievable for multiple years. These show amounts and vendors even without itemized receipts.

**Get duplicates.** Many vendors, hotels, and airlines can provide copies of receipts or invoices from prior years. Software companies and subscription services almost always have billing history available in your account.

**Use secondary evidence.** Calendar entries showing meetings, client emails discussing projects, photos of equipment, project files showing dates of work. All of these corroborate that real business activity occurred.

**Document your reconstruction.** Write down exactly what you are doing to recreate the records and why the originals are unavailable. A fire, flood, hard drive failure, or other credible explanation for missing records is better than no explanation.

**Be conservative on estimates.** Courts applying the Cohan Rule tend to estimate on the low end. A claim that seems inflated relative to your income or business type will not survive. Come in with a number that looks defensible and reasonable, not one that maximizes the deduction.

## The Practical Lesson

The Cohan Rule is a fallback, not a strategy. It is considerably harder to use successfully than having actual receipts, and for the four Section 274(d) categories, it does not apply at all.

The best version of this story is one where you never need to invoke it. Keep receipts, use an expense app that photographs them immediately, and back up your records somewhere that survives a laptop dying.

George Cohan won his case, but he also spent years fighting the IRS over it. That is not a great use of anyone's time.
