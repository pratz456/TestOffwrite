New freelancers discover self-employment tax the same way everyone discovers they have been doing something wrong for years: with a jarring tax bill and a sinking feeling.

You expected to pay income tax. You knew about that. What nobody mentioned was the extra **15.3%** that lands before income tax even enters the conversation.

## The Double Taxation Nobody Warned You About

When you work for an employer, your paycheck shows Social Security and Medicare taxes being withheld. What the paycheck does not show is that your employer is also paying the exact same amount again, matched dollar for dollar, out of their own pocket. You only see half the bill.

Go freelance and you see both halves. Self-employment tax is the combined 15.3% that was always being paid on your labor. The difference is that previously your employer covered half. Now you are the employer.

Here is how the 15.3% breaks down:

- **Social Security:** 12.4% on net self-employment income up to $176,100 (2025 wage base)
- **Medicare:** 2.9% on all net self-employment income with no cap
- **Additional Medicare Tax:** 0.9% on net earnings above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly)

## How It Gets Calculated

Self-employment tax does not apply to your gross income. It applies to **net self-employment income**, which is your business profit after deductible expenses. Then there is one more adjustment before the math runs.

The IRS lets you multiply net self-employment income by **92.35%** before applying the tax rate. This is because employers deduct their half of payroll taxes as a business expense. The 92.35% factor gives you the equivalent treatment. So you are not actually paying 15.3% of your full net profit. You are paying 15.3% of 92.35% of your net profit, which works out to roughly 14.13% effective rate.

The calculation runs on **Schedule SE**, which flows from your Schedule C.

### A Real Example

Net self-employment income from Schedule C: $60,000

Step 1: $60,000 x 92.35% = $55,410
Step 2: $55,410 x 15.3% = $8,478 in self-employment tax

That $8,478 then shows up in two places. Half of it ($4,239) is deductible as an above-the-line deduction on Schedule 1. That deduction lowers your adjusted gross income, which lowers your income tax. You do not get the money back, but you do get a partial offset.

## Why Freelancers Get Blindsided

W-2 employees see payroll tax on every check. They live with it continuously throughout the year and it is factored into their mental model of their take-home pay.

Freelancers often receive full payment, spend most of it, and then discover at tax time that 15.3% of every dollar was effectively spoken for before income tax even applied. On $80,000 in net self-employment income, that is roughly $11,300 in self-employment tax sitting on top of whatever income tax is owed.

This is the single most common reason new freelancers end up owing amounts they cannot pay.

The solution is mechanical: set aside money for self-employment tax as income arrives, not after the year ends.

## Two Legal Ways to Reduce It

**Strategy 1: Reduce net profit with real deductions.** Self-employment tax applies to net profit after business expenses. Every legitimate deduction reduces both income tax and self-employment tax. A $1,000 deduction for someone in the 22% bracket saves $220 in income tax plus roughly $141 in SE tax for a combined $361 savings.

**Strategy 2: Elect S-corporation status.** An S-corp pays you a reasonable W-2 salary and passes remaining profit as a distribution. Distributions are not subject to self-employment tax. Only the W-2 salary goes through payroll taxes.

On $120,000 in net profit, if you pay yourself a $70,000 salary and take $50,000 as a distribution, you pay payroll taxes only on $70,000 instead of the full $120,000. The $50,000 distribution avoids self-employment tax entirely, which saves roughly $7,000. That has to be weighed against the cost of running payroll and filing a separate corporate return, but at higher income levels the math works strongly in favor of the S-corp.

Self-employment tax is not optional and it is not going away. The best approach is to understand it clearly, account for it immediately when income arrives, and look at legitimate structural strategies if your income reaches levels where they make sense.
