Congratulations. You left the job, landed some clients, and started getting paid. Then April arrives and your accountant says you owe $8,400. You do not have $8,400. Nobody mentioned this part.

This guide exists because the tax system treats employees and self-employed workers completely differently, and the transition blindsides almost everyone. Here is the full picture before it surprises you.

## The Two Bills You Did Not Know About

When you work for an employer, taxes happen quietly. Federal income tax gets withheld from every paycheck. Social Security and Medicare get withheld too. You file in April, maybe get a refund, life moves on.

Go self-employed and the system flips. Nobody withholds anything. Every dollar that hits your bank account is pre-tax. And you now owe two separate things:

**Self-employment tax: 15.3%**
This is Social Security (12.4%) and Medicare (2.9%) combined. Your employer used to pay half of this : 7.65% : invisibly. Now you pay both halves. On $60,000 in net profit that is $9,180 before federal income tax is even calculated.

**Federal income tax: your bracket rate**
After SE tax, your net profit goes through the standard income tax brackets. A single filer earning $60,000 net pays roughly $6,700 in federal income tax on top of the SE tax.

Total federal obligation on $60,000 net: roughly $15,750. That is 25% of every dollar you earn, and none of it is automatically withheld.

## The Quarterly Payment Requirement

The IRS does not want to wait until April. If you expect to owe more than $1,000 in federal taxes for the year, you are legally required to make estimated tax payments four times a year.

The deadlines are:
- **April 15** : for income earned January through March
- **June 16** : for income earned April through May
- **September 15** : for income earned June through August
- **January 15** : for income earned September through December

Miss these and the IRS charges an underpayment penalty calculated on what you should have paid and when. It is not a huge fine but it is entirely avoidable.

**The safe harbor rule:** If you pay either 90% of this year's tax liability OR 100% of last year's total tax (110% if last year's AGI was above $150,000), you avoid the underpayment penalty entirely regardless of what you actually owe in April.

For first-year freelancers, last year you probably had zero self-employment tax. So your safe harbor baseline is low. Focus on the 90% of current year liability approach and estimate your income honestly.

## How to Estimate What to Set Aside

A rough starting formula that works for most freelancers:

Take every dollar that comes in. Multiply by 92.35% (this adjusts for the SE tax deduction). Then apply 15.3% to that number. Add your expected income tax at your bracket rate. Divide by 12 and move that amount to a tax savings account every single month.

For most people in the middle brackets, setting aside **25 to 30% of every payment** keeps you covered. Set it aside immediately when the money arrives, not later.

## What Actually Reduces Your Bill

The single most important concept in freelance taxes: the IRS taxes your **net profit**, not your gross revenue. Every legitimate business expense reduces both your income tax and your SE tax bill.

A $1,000 deduction for a freelancer in the 22% bracket saves:
- $141 in SE tax (because it reduces net profit)
- $220 in income tax
- **$361 total** from a single $1,000 expense

This is why tracking every business expense matters immediately. Not at year-end. From the very first invoice.

Expenses that new freelancers routinely miss:
- Software subscriptions (Adobe, Notion, Slack, Zoom, every SaaS tool)
- Home office (dedicated space in your home used regularly and exclusively for work)
- Phone and internet (the business use percentage)
- Professional development (courses, certifications, books)
- Health insurance premiums (fully deductible above the line if you are not eligible for employer coverage)
- Retirement contributions (SEP-IRA allows up to 25% of net income)

## The First Actions to Take Right Now

**Open a separate business bank account.** Free options like Mercury and Relay take 10 minutes. Every client payment goes here. Every business expense comes out of here. This creates a clean record and makes tax time a document review instead of an archaeology project.

**Decide on accounting software.** Wave is free and connects to your bank. QuickBooks Self-Employed is $15 a month. Either works. The goal is that every transaction gets categorized as it happens, not reconstructed from memory in March.

**Mark your quarterly due dates.** Put all four in your calendar today with a reminder two weeks before each one. The first one that catches you off guard costs you a penalty. The ones after that are optional : but only if you were never surprised by the first one.

**Understand your Schedule C.** This is the form where your business income and expenses are reconciled. Net profit on Schedule C flows to your Form 1040 and becomes the basis for both your SE tax and your income tax. Every business decision you make is ultimately a Schedule C decision.

First year freelancing is genuinely harder from a tax perspective than any year after it. The second year you know what is coming. The third year you are planning around it. The first year is when people get hurt. Now you have been warned.
