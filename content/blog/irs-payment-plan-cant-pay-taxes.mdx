Every year a meaningful percentage of freelancers file their return accurately, discover they owe more than they expected, and do not have the money sitting in an account. Sometimes it is a bad income year. Sometimes it is good income without enough set aside. Either way, the balance is real and the options are finite.

Here is the honest breakdown of what you can do.

## Option 1: Pay Late With Penalties and Interest

Not paying on time does not trigger criminal consequences if you filed correctly. It triggers financial ones.

The IRS charges two things when you owe and do not pay:

**Failure-to-pay penalty:** 0.5% of the unpaid balance per month, up to a maximum of 25%.

**Interest:** Currently around 8% annually (it is the federal short-term rate plus 3%, adjusted quarterly), compounding daily on both the tax and the penalties.

On a $10,000 balance, that is roughly $80 per month in interest alone before penalties. Not catastrophic, but it adds up and does not stop accruing until paid.

## Option 2: Short-Term Payment Extension

If you can pay in full within 180 days, the IRS offers an online short-term payment plan with no setup fee. You still owe interest and the failure-to-pay penalty, but there is no installment agreement fee and no formal arrangement to maintain.

Apply at IRS.gov using the Online Payment Agreement tool. Most people are approved instantly.

## Option 3: Installment Agreement

Cannot pay within 180 days but can pay monthly? An installment agreement lets you spread payments over up to 72 months.

**Streamlined installment agreement:** If you owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties, and interest, and can pay in full within 72 months, you can apply online with automatic approval. No financial disclosure required. Setup fee is $31 if you use direct debit, $130 otherwise.

**Non-streamlined:** Balances over $50,000 require submitting financial information. The IRS reviews income and expenses to determine what you can pay.

While under an installment agreement: penalties and interest continue to accrue, but the failure-to-pay penalty rate drops from 0.5% to 0.25% per month once you have an agreement in place.

## Option 4: Currently Not Collectible

If you genuinely cannot afford to pay and paying would leave you unable to cover basic living expenses, the IRS can place your account in **Currently Not Collectible (CNC) status.** This temporarily pauses collection activity.

What CNC does: stops levies and collection letters, buys time without active enforcement.

What CNC does not do: stop interest and penalties from accruing, forgive any debt, or permanently resolve the issue.

The IRS reviews CNC status periodically and will resume collection if your financial situation improves. This is a pause button, not an exit.

To request CNC status, you typically submit Form 433-A or 433-F (Collection Information Statement) showing your income and expenses. The IRS uses national and local standards to determine what counts as reasonable living expenses.

## Option 5: Offer in Compromise

An Offer in Compromise (OIC) lets you settle your tax debt for less than the full amount owed. The IRS accepts offers when the amount offered represents the most they can reasonably expect to collect given your financial circumstances.

This sounds better than it is in practice. The IRS accepts roughly 30-40% of OIC applications, and the process takes 12-24 months. You must be current on all filing and payment obligations to apply, including estimated tax payments.

The IRS calculates your minimum acceptable offer based on your "reasonable collection potential": essentially your available equity in assets plus a multiple of your monthly disposable income. If you have significant assets or income, the minimum offer may not be much less than the full amount.

OICs make the most sense when you genuinely cannot pay, have limited assets, and the tax debt is significantly larger than your collection potential.

## What Happens If You Do Nothing

The IRS will eventually: send multiple notices, file a federal tax lien (which appears on public record and damages credit), issue levies against bank accounts and receivables, and in serious long-term cases, pursue wage garnishment.

The collection process takes time, but it is relentless and automatic. A balance does not expire during normal compliance (the 10-year collection statute has many pauses). Ignoring notices does not make the debt go away.

## The Actual Best Path

File your return even if you cannot pay. The failure-to-file penalty is 10 times larger than the failure-to-pay penalty (5% per month versus 0.5%). Owing money is manageable. Owing money plus a large penalty for not filing is worse.

Then set up the simplest payment arrangement that works for your situation. The IRS would rather receive monthly payments than deal with collections. The online tools at IRS.gov handle most straightforward cases without needing a professional or calling anyone.

If your balance is large, your situation is complicated, or you are already receiving levy notices, a tax professional is worth consulting. The fee is predictable. The cost of an unmanaged IRS collection is not.
