Working from a laptop while traveling sounds glamorous until April arrives and you realize you may owe income tax to four different states, some of which you visited for two weeks combined.

Multi-state taxation is genuinely complicated, the rules vary wildly by state, and getting it wrong means either overpaying or underpaying, neither of which ends well.

## The Basic Rule: Where You Work Matters

Most states tax income based on where the work is **performed**, not where you live. If you spend time working in a state, that state generally wants a piece of the income you earned there.

For a freelancer spending three months working from a rented Airbnb in Colorado and nine months working from their home state of Texas, Colorado may want tax on the income earned during those three months.

Texas has no income tax. Colorado does. Welcome to the complication.

## What Makes You a Tax Resident

Every state has rules for what constitutes residency, and being a tax resident is more serious than being a temporary visitor. Common thresholds:

**Statutory residency:** Some states (New York is the most aggressive) will consider you a statutory resident if you maintain a "permanent place of abode" there and spend more than 183 days in the state during the year, regardless of where you intend to be domiciled.

**Domicile:** Your primary, permanent home. You can only have one domicile, but you can be a statutory resident of multiple states simultaneously, which means potentially full state income tax in multiple places on the same income.

**Nonresident with income sourced to the state:** Even without residency, you may owe tax as a nonresident on income earned within state borders.

## How Credits Prevent Double Taxation (Usually)

Most states offer a **credit for taxes paid to other states** to prevent you from paying full tax twice on the same income. Your home state generally credits you for income tax you paid to other states on the same income.

The credit usually works cleanly when states have comparable tax rates. It works less cleanly when:

- Your home state has a higher rate than the other state (you pay the difference)
- Your home state has no income tax (there is no credit mechanism because there is no home state tax to offset)
- The states have conflicting rules about what income is sourced where

## The Convenience of the Employer Doctrine

This rule applies to employees, but it illustrates how aggressively some states approach the issue. New York, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Nebraska follow the "convenience of the employer" rule, which says that if a remote worker works from another state for their own convenience (rather than because the employer required it), the income is still taxed as if earned in the employer's state.

For S-corp owners with employees, or freelancers who work partially from a state with this rule, the implications can be significant.

## Which States Are Most Aggressive

**New York** is the most aggressive state in the country for taxing nonresidents and applying statutory residency rules. The state actively audits people who claim to have left and pursues income earned by former residents.

**California** similarly pursues income earned by people who worked in the state or have business connections there. California will tax stock options and deferred compensation connected to California employment even after you have moved.

**Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Nebraska** apply the convenience rule for employee income.

States with **no income tax** (Florida, Texas, Nevada, Washington, Wyoming, South Dakota, Alaska, Tennessee, New Hampshire) are popular destinations for high-income earners looking to establish domicile. The move has to be genuine and well-documented, or former home states will argue you never really left.

## What You Need to Track

If you work from multiple states during the year, keep records of:

- The specific days worked in each state (a calendar or travel log)
- The income attributable to work performed in each state
- Any receipts, apartment leases, or records establishing your physical presence

For service businesses, income is generally sourced to the state where the services are performed. If you split your time between states, you split the income proportionally.

## Filing Requirements

Working in multiple states typically means filing a nonresident or part-year resident return in each state where you earned income above that state's filing threshold, plus your home state return.

Most states have a minimum threshold below which filing is not required (often $1,000-$2,000 in state-sourced income). If you spent one week working in a state and earned $800 of income attributable to that work, many states do not require a return.

Above the thresholds, filing is required. Reciprocity agreements between some neighboring states simplify this: certain state pairs agree to tax workers only in their home state, which eliminates the nonresident filing in the other state. These agreements exist in specific pairs and are worth checking if you frequently cross a particular state border.

## When to Get Help

If you are earning significant income in multiple states, a tax professional familiar with multi-state allocation is worth the cost. The filing requirements and credit calculations can interact in non-obvious ways, and an error in one state can cascade to incorrect credits in another.

The complexity scales with how many states you work in, the relative tax rates, and whether you are dealing with statutory residency risk anywhere. A straightforward case with two states and matching credit rules is manageable. Four states with conflicting sourcing rules and a potential New York residency argument is not a DIY project.
