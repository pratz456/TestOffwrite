In 1962, $25 was a reasonably generous gift. A nice dinner. A decent bottle of something. The Apollo 1 disaster hadn't happened yet, and Congress apparently decided that $25 per person per year was the right limit for deductible business gifts.

It is now 2026. The limit is still $25. Adjusted for inflation, that 1962 $25 would be worth over $260 today. Congress has simply chosen not to care.

So here are the rules for a deduction that hasn't kept up with roughly 60 years of price increases.

## The Rule

You can deduct **up to $25 per recipient per year** for business gifts. That's $25 total per person, across all gifts you give them during the year. Not $25 per gift. Per person.

The gift must be given in connection with your trade or business.

Gifts to a company (rather than an individual at a company) that will be used by multiple employees are subject to a separate analysis, but the $25 limit applies to any gift that would go to a particular individual.

## What Counts as a Gift

A business gift is something given without expectation of immediate return. This is where the gift vs. entertainment distinction matters a lot:

**Gifts:** wine, gift cards, books, branded merchandise, flowers, food items sent to a client's office, tickets to an event you're not attending with them.

**Not gifts (entertainment):** tickets to an event you both attend together (that's entertainment, and entertainment is 0% deductible since 2018), meals where you're both present (that's a business meal at 50%).

If you give a client two tickets to a concert and also attend with them, the whole thing is entertainment: $0 deductible. If you give them the tickets and don't go, it's a gift: $25 deductible per ticket recipient.

The same item can change categories depending on whether you show up.

## Incidental Costs

Incidental costs like gift wrapping, engraving, or mailing don't count toward the $25 limit. A $25 gift with $8 in shipping and a $5 engraving job remains a deductible $25 gift (plus the incidental costs as separate deductions).

## The Promotional Materials Exception

Items that cost $4 or less, have your company name on them, and are given widely (pens, notepads, tote bags, stickers) are **not subject to the $25 limit**. These are promotional items, not gifts. Deduct the full cost.

Similarly, signs, display racks, and materials that stay at the customer's business (a branded poster, a counter display) are not gifts and have no dollar limit.

## Gifts to Employees

The gift rules also apply to gifts to your own employees. Gifts of cash or cash equivalents (gift cards, checks, certificates redeemable for cash) are always taxable wages, regardless of amount.

Non-cash gifts to employees under the "de minimis fringe benefit" rule can be excluded from their income if they're occasional, small, and of a type that accounting for them would be unreasonable. The IRS examples: occasional holiday turkeys, fruit baskets, event tickets for infrequent occasions.

As a practical matter, most meaningful non-cash gifts to employees above $100 will be treated as taxable compensation, not excludable fringe benefits.

## Documentation

For gifts over $25, you can only deduct $25, but you should still document the full picture:

- Cost of the gift
- Date given
- Name and business relationship of recipient
- Business purpose for the gift

If a gift could be interpreted as either a gift or entertainment, the documentation of your intent and the circumstances matters for determining which rules apply.

## The Reality Check

For most freelancers, the business gift deduction is a minor line item. At $25 per person, even a client list of 20 people generates a maximum deduction of $500. At a 25% effective rate, that's $125 in tax savings.

The practical lesson isn't to obsess over the $25 limit but to know it exists. Spending $200 on a client gift is a generous gesture. Just understand that $175 of it is coming out of your post-tax pocket, and only $25 makes it onto your Schedule C.

Some things in the tax code are opportunities to exploit. The $25 gift limit is mostly a constraint to be aware of. And a reminder that Congress set this number when a gallon of gas cost 28 cents, and has shown no interest in updating it since.
