Freelance designers usually file as sole proprietors or single-member LLCs, so business income and expenses go on **Schedule C**. Every legitimate business expense lowers both your income tax and the 15.3% self-employment tax, which makes it worth knowing what counts.

The test is the same for every deduction: the expense must be **ordinary and necessary** for your design business (IRC §162). A personal cost doesn't become a business cost because you use it while working, and mixed-use costs need a reasonable business-use percentage.

## Software, fonts and assets

For most designers, tools are the largest recurring expense:

- Design and prototyping software (Adobe Creative Cloud, Figma, Sketch, Canva, Affinity)
- Font licenses, stock photos, icons, mockups and plugins
- Project and client tools (Notion, Slack, Zoom, invoicing software)
- Portfolio hosting, domains and website builders

Subscriptions used only for your business are deductible in the year you pay for them. If a subscription is shared with personal use, such as a family plan, deduct only the business share.

## Computers, tablets and other equipment

Laptops, monitors, drawing tablets, cameras and color-calibration tools are business assets. There are three common ways to deduct them:

- **De minimis safe harbor.** If you make the annual election, you can expense items costing up to **$2,500 per item or invoice** instead of depreciating them (the limit for businesses without audited financial statements).
- **Section 179.** Deduct the business-use cost of qualifying equipment in the year you place it in service, up to **$2,560,000** for 2026. The deduction can't exceed your business income for the year; the excess carries forward.
- **Bonus depreciation.** Generally **100%** for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025.

Record the purchase date, cost and business-use percentage. If you use a laptop 70% for client work, 70% of its cost is the starting point. Our [Section 179 guide](/blog/section-179-equipment-deduction-guide) covers the choices in more detail.

## Home office

If you use a specific area of your home **regularly and exclusively** as your principal place of business, you can deduct home office costs (Publication 587). A desk in a spare room qualifies if nothing else happens there; a dining table used for family dinners doesn't.

- **Simplified method:** $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet ($1,500).
- **Regular method:** the business percentage of rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance and repairs, calculated on Form 8829.

Administrative work such as invoicing, scheduling and bookkeeping counts toward principal place of business even if you also work at client offices, as long as there's no other fixed location where you do substantial administrative work. See our [home office guide](/blog/home-office-deduction-guide).

## Internet and phone

Deduct the business-use share of your internet service and phone. An estimate backed by a sample of your actual usage is easier to support than a round number.

## Contractors and collaborators

Paying an illustrator, developer, copywriter or production artist is a deductible business expense. Collect a **Form W-9** before you pay. For payments made in 2026, you generally file **Form 1099-NEC** for a contractor you pay **$2,000 or more**; the threshold was $600 for payments made in 2025. Payments by card or through a payment app are generally reported by the payment processor instead.

## Education and professional memberships

Courses, workshops, books and conference fees that **maintain or improve skills** you use in your current design business are deductible (Treas. Reg. §1.162-5). Education that qualifies you for a new trade isn't. Dues for professional organizations such as AIGA are generally deductible; dues for social or athletic clubs aren't.

## Coworking, marketing and client costs

- **Coworking memberships** and studio rent.
- **Marketing:** portfolio site costs, Behance or Dribbble Pro, paid ads, business cards and printed samples.
- **Client meals:** generally **50%** deductible when you're present, the meal isn't lavish and it has a business purpose. Entertainment, such as event tickets, isn't deductible.
- **Travel:** overnight trips away from your tax home for client work or conferences, including airfare, lodging and 50% of meals.

## Deductions outside Schedule C that still matter

- **Self-employment tax:** deduct half of it on Schedule 1.
- **Health insurance:** the self-employed health insurance deduction, if you aren't eligible for a subsidized plan through an employer, including a spouse's.
- **Retirement:** SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) contributions, with a combined 2026 limit of $72,000. See our [retirement guide](/blog/retirement-accounts-self-employed).
- **QBI deduction:** up to 20% of qualified business income. Below 2026 taxable income of **$201,750** ($403,500 on a joint return), the type of business doesn't limit the deduction.

## Quarterly estimated taxes

Irregular client payments make it easy to underpay during the year. If you expect to owe at least $1,000, pay estimated tax by April 15, June 15 and September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027. Paying 100% of last year's tax (110% if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000) generally avoids the underpayment penalty. Our [quarterly estimate calculator](/tools/quarterly-estimate-calculator) gives a starting figure.

## Records to keep

For each expense, keep the receipt or invoice, the date, the amount, what it was for, and the client or project if it relates to one. For equipment, add the purchase date and business-use percentage. The IRS generally expects records for at least three years after you file.
